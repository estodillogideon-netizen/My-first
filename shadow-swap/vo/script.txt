In my world, your shadow shows how you really feel.
Happy people? Their shadows dance. Liars? Their shadows cross their fingers.
Me? I'm Kai. I smile all day. And my shadow hasn't stopped crying in a year.
Every morning, Mom asks, "How are you, Kai?" I say, "I'm fine." And behind me, my shadow sobs into its hands.
At school, every shadow does something.
Jay's shadow flexes. Priya's shadow blushes. Even the teacher's shadow yawns.
Mine just sits on the floor and cries. In front of everyone.
So I learned some tricks.
I stand in the shade. I hide behind lockers. I eat lunch under the stairs.
No light. No shadow. No questions.
Only one person never stares. Mr. Ollie, the janitor.
Every day he mops the hall, humming the same old song.
And every day, there's a note on my locker. "Rough day?" "Hang in there."
I never wrote back.
Then on Friday, the power went out.
Everyone went outside. Into the sun. Every shadow, out in the open.
Kids pointed at mine. Somebody laughed.
I wanted to disappear.
Then Mr. Ollie walked out and stood right next to me, in the sunlight.
And that's when I saw it.
Mr. Ollie has no shadow. None. Not even a little one by his shoes.
"Where's your shadow?" I asked.
He leaned on his mop and smiled. "I gave it away. A long time ago."
"There was a boy at this school. His sadness got so heavy, he couldn't get up."
"So I gave him my shadow. It carried the sad part for him."
"When he grew up, it stayed with his family."
He looked at my shadow. Then at me. "You smile just like your father, Kai."
That boy was my dad.
Dad passed away last spring. And I never cried. I just smiled, so nobody would ask.
But my shadow knew. It cried every single day. For me.
Mr. Ollie knelt down. "How are you, Kai? Really?"
And for the first time, I didn't say fine.
"I'm not okay," I said. "I miss my dad."
And then I cried. Real tears. Big, ugly ones.
And behind me, my shadow stopped crying.
It wiped its face. It stood up. And it walked straight to Mr. Ollie.
And it hugged him. After thirty years, it was finally home.
Then I looked down. At my feet, there was a new shadow. Small. Mine.
Two shadows, side by side. Holding hands.
Now when Mom asks how I am, I tell her the truth.
Some days, my shadow still cries. But now, I cry with it.
Turns out, a shadow gets lighter when you share it.
